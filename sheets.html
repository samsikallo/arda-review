The Drawn Sheets

Middle-earth and Beleriand traced one path per pen stroke, drawn into one page at one scale, with the hand that drew each plate named on it
The Maps of Arda. the combined sheet · the gazetteer · Aman · the marked sheet

One hall, three views and a register, and each says whose hand it is. The combined sheet is Christopher Tolkien’s linework, measured as centrelines by this archive’s own instrument. The gazetteer is what the corpus knows, and it holds 481 places — several hundred more than the traced sheet can show. Aman is the archive’s own drawing, and there is no plate of it — the Undying Lands were never mapped in the published books as Middle-earth was. Why the rest of the gazetteer is not pinned to this sheet: a pin here must sit where the archive read it on one of his plates, and the archive’s own drawing is in a different frame that does not register to them — measured, not assumed, and set out below. Until a place is anchored to a plate by hand, the gazetteer is where it lives.

2 plates, 103,001 centrelines, drawn into one page at one scale. Each sheet is traced one path per pen stroke — not shown as a picture — by the same instrument and at the same settings that produced the Marked Sheet. The hand that drew each plate is named on it. The placement is MEASURED, not judged by eye. Each plate's linework was correlated against Middle-earth and Beleriand Map (2) over an exhaustive search of scale and offset, refined to a single pixel: Beleriand at ×0.613, the General Map at ×1.460 offset (792, −257). And the composite SPLICES the two plates rather than superimposing them. Ered Luin is one range and Tolkien drew it on both sheets — so the two drawings ought to coincide — but in this composite Beleriand’s own Ossiriand, Thargelion and Ered Luin were cut away, and what stands east of Lothlann and Estolad is the General Map’s Lindon: the Gulf of Lhûn, Forlindon, Mithlond, Harlindon. The two drawings of the range never meet anywhere on this sheet. The crossover is not an edge but a curve — the ink is Beleriand’s out to about x 780 and the General Map’s beyond x 907. The General Map plate is also a different edition from the one composed here — no ocean hachuring, a square compass cartouche where the reference has a rose, different lettering, and it runs south to Far Harad, which is why its fit is good rather than exact (±5 px, ±0.5%). His lettering can be turned off, and it took three attempts to earn that. Telling a letter from a line by shape failed on a fact about this map — a row of small dense marks on a baseline is a range of hill glyphs and it is a line of text. Using the label boxes worked topologically but their upright rectangles broke Ered Wethrin, holed the Forest of Brethil and erased the Birchwoods of Arvernien, because Tolkien letters on slants and curves. What works is a mask fitted to his red ink — one minimum-area oriented rectangle per glyph, so the mask runs with the word instead of spanning it — and a path is dropped only when its entire connected component lies inside it, which is why a river passing under a name survives. The traced plates are untouched and remain what this page draws by default. The title cartouche and the scale bar stay in both: they are drawn in black, and they are part of the drawing rather than names on the land.
Two drawings and a register, and each is a different kind of evidence. This sheet shows what Tolkien's hand drew — traced from the plates, one path per pen stroke. The gazetteer holds what the corpus knows: 481 places, every one named, cited and reachable, against the 160 this sheet can pin — because a pin here must sit where the archive read it on a plate, and most places were never on one. The map of Aman is the archive's own drawing, from text and measurement. None of the three replaces another, and collapsing them would tell you they are the same thing. Aman is not on this sheet, and it is not an omission. The archive holds eight traced plates — Beleriand, the General Map, Gondor and Rohan, the realms of the Noldor, Númenor, the Shire, Wilderland and Thrór's map — and none of them draws Aman. There is no plate to trace, because the Undying Lands were never mapped in the published books the way Middle-earth was. The archive draws Aman itself, from the corpus and from measurement — that is the map of Aman, and it stays a page of its own rather than being folded in here, because a drawing made from text and a tracing made from ink are two different kinds of evidence and the reader is owed the difference.

Named, and not placed on this sheet.

This sheet carries 161 of his labels, which are 160 distinct places, because he letters South Gondor twice on the same sheet. The archive holds 481. The difference is 321 named places the archive holds and this sheet does not draw, and that number belongs on the page rather than in a commit message.

The reason is the plates, not the archive. A pin on this sheet sits where Christopher Tolkien’s own hand lettered a name, measured off the plate he lettered it on. He lettered 160 of the archive’s places on these two sheets, and every one of those is already here. The rest he never lettered on these plates — so in this frame they have no position at all, and no amount of work on this page can give them one.

The archive holds a coordinate for 236 of them, and it is the wrong coordinate to use. Those live in the frame of the archive’s own drawing, not in the frame of his plates. Whether the two frames can be brought into register is a question with an answer, and the answer was measured rather than assumed: a transform was fitted on every place whose position this archive knows both ways, then tested by leaving each place out in turn and asking the transform to predict the one it had not seen.

  • The Third Age drawing, tested against 71 places whose position this archive knows both ways: typically 141 px out, and at worst 757 px.
  • The First Age drawing, tested against 52 places whose position this archive knows both ways: typically 71 px out, and at worst 304 px.

The pins already on this sheet are good to about five pixels. A place carried across by that transform would land tens or hundreds of pixels from where it belongs — far enough, at the worst of it, to sit in a different region of Middle-earth. So the archive does not carry them across. A coordinate produced that way would look exactly like the measured ones beside it and would not be one, and a drawing that cannot be told apart from a guess is the thing this archive is built against. The remaining 85 have no coordinate in any frame, and the archive does not invent one for those either.

Nothing here is lost, and that is the other half. Every one of these places is published, named, cited and reachable — in the gazetteer, and each on its own place page with what the corpus says about it. They are absent from this drawing, which is a statement about two plates, and not from the archive.

The map of Aman is the archive’s own, and that is the point.

Aman drawn from the corpus by the Arda Archive. No map of this land exists in Christopher Tolkien's hand; the outline, the coastline and every coordinate here are the archive's own work and carry no authority but their own. What the corpus establishes is the RELATION recorded on each feature, never the position.

There is no plate of Christopher Tolkien’s hand for Aman — the Undying Lands were never mapped in the published books as Middle-earth was. What the corpus gives is always a relation — north of, behind, in the midst of — and never a coordinate. The coordinates are ours: one solution to those relations, not a discovery.

26 features are placed, each carrying the passage that places it. 4 of the 26 are placed by inference, and each says so.

  • Taniquetil — the corpus fixes Taniquetil THREE times: the highest of the Pelóri; 'standing upon the margin of the sea' (Silm:2732); and — the one that places it ALONG the range — 'Túna beneath Taniquetil' (Silm, Flight of the Noldor) [C], where Túna is 'The green hill in the Calacirya' by Silm's own index [C]. Drawn beside the pass on those two, not on the Vanyar inference given here until 12 Aug. t1_a_the_silmarillion.txt:2637.
  • the Máhanaxar — 'near to the golden gates' fixes it beside the city but not on which side; it is drawn by Ezellohar because the Valar sat in it to hear Yavanna sing upon the mound. t1_a_the_silmarillion.txt:2663.
  • Mandos — the published Silmarillion reads 'westward' and Christopher Tolkien calls that his own change and 'a regrettable error'; the final text of the Valaquenta has 'northward'. Placed north-of-centre beside Formenos because Feanor 'dwelt northward in Valinor near to the halls of Mandos'. The compass direction is Tolkien's final text; the exact spot is the archive's. t1_10_morgoths_ring.txt:7784.
  • Lórellin — the corpus places Lórellin IN Lórien, and Lórien only 'in the land of the Valar'. Where in that land is nowhere stated. It is drawn on the plain south of Valmar as a position that contradicts nothing, and for no better reason than that. t1_a_the_silmarillion.txt:2338.

Named, and nowhere placed. These 2 are not on the map, and their absence is the most careful thing on this page.

  • the gardens of Lórien — 'in the land of the Valar' is the whole of it. Lórien is placed nowhere within Valinor, and the lake of Lórellin is placed only within Lórien. “In Lórien are his gardens in the land of the Valar, and they are the fairest of all places in the world” t1_a_the_silmarillion.txt:2334
  • the woods of Oromë — Oromë's woods in Aman are named but never sited; what the corpus places is his riding in Middle-earth, which is a different thing. “Oromë loved the lands of Middle-earth, and he left them unwillingly” t1_a_the_silmarillion.txt:2376

The other six plates

The archive traced eight plates and this page drew two. The other six were tracked, published, reachable by URL and rendered by no page at all — 101,353 centrelines, 47% of the archive's traced linework, sitting on the server for nobody. They are here now. Each is shown on its own, in its own frame, and that is deliberate: Beleriand and the General Map share one space because their placement was measured, by correlation. Nothing has measured where Gondor-and-Rohan or Wilderland belong against them, and placing them by eye is what produced two wrong placements earlier today.

The places the archive holds and does not draw

The archive holds a coordinate for places its own sheet never marks, and this is where it says so. 145 records carry a point that the archive’s reference drawing does not draw — Anfalas, Anfauglith, Angband, Angmar, Anórien, Ard-galen, the Argonath and a hundred and thirty-eight more. The archive knew where they were and put none of them on its map. This layer draws every one of them that falls on the sheet. It is drawn on the archive’s OWN sheet and not on Christopher Tolkien’s plates above, because those coordinates are in the archive’s frame, and this page has already measured what carrying them into the plate frame would cost — typically 141 px out and at worst 757 px, far enough to sit in a different region of Middle-earth. That refusal stands, and the layer is shown in the frame the coordinates are actually in rather than moved into one where they would be wrong. The base sheet is not altered. The marks are drawn above it and never into it: the drawing keeps a loremaster’s restraint, and a hundred and forty-five more marks would make it another kind of map altogether. One mark per point, and the mark says how firmly it is placed. A record whose point is its own is drawn solid; one that borrowed the point of the place containing it, or was set down by a measure, is drawn hollow and dashed. Seven points carry more than one record — Khazad-dûm, its Bridge and its West-gate all sit at Caradhras’ point, because that is the only point any text gives them — and drawing a mark each would invent positions out of one. The card names everything the archive puts there. The archive’s reference sheet is 403 KB and is fetched only when you ask for this layer, so a reader who never opens it pays nothing for it — the same terms as the six plates above.
