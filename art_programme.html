3 · The idiom the archive draws in

This block is appended to every place prompt. It is the archive’s own words about a drawing style — it is not Tolkien and it claims nothing about any place. Read it, because if the idiom is wrong then the 141 are wrong before a single one is written.

A hand-drawn film-workshop ENVIRONMENT DESIGN plate, traditional pen-and-ink with watercolour and marker washes on warm off-white cream paper, like a physical concept sketch scanned from an artist's sketchbook. Drawn with thin, confident dark sepia-brown pen linework and DENSE hand-inked cross-hatching for every shadow; obsessive hand-drawn surface detail rendered individually with fine ink strokes. Colour laid on like real watercolour and felt-tip markers: soft, muted, slightly desaturated tones that streak, pool and mottle unevenly, never a flat digital fill; paper texture shows through. Muted earthy palette. Cel-style shading kept simple — one mid-tone, darker cross-hatched shadows, a few pale highlights. A short handwritten flowing calligraphic ink caption sits near the bottom of the page. Looks like traditional media on paper — NOT vector art, NOT a cel-shaded cartoon, NOT a glossy 3D render, NOT a smooth digital painting, NO gradients, NO glow, NO neon, NO thick uniform outlines. A SINGLE piece of architecture drawn as a study on the page — the structure large and centred and fully visible, with only a faint sketchy indication of the ground it stands on and a little loose hatching or a pale wash at its base; the rest of the page left as bare warm paper. NOT a finished landscape painting, NO sky, NO clouds, NO sunset, NO full scenery — an architectural concept study, with tiny simplified figures for scale where useful. Believable buildable stonework, structurally sound.

Every plate in this idiom the archive holds. 6 architectural views, against 144 built places — that is the ratio that matters — plus 4 creature plates, which are drawn to the same idiom and are not views of a place and are not in that denominator. This is the whole of what you have been shown so far, and it is section 5’s “the plate it would produce”.

5 · The form, on three real placesC254 order

For each: what the corpus actually holds, then a prompt built from it by word match only — the archive composes no sentence about what the place looked like — then the plate. Seven slots, because a picture of a building decides all seven whether the corpus speaks or not; naming the slot turns a silent invention into a declared one. A green mark is a word found in an attributed passage, and the passage is printed so you can check the match rather than trust it.

Marks: [C] Tolkien’s narrative · [C-idx] Christopher Tolkien’s index gloss · [SILENCE] the corpus does not say · [ARCHIVE] the archive’s own voice, claiming nothing.

Read the passages, because the match is only a word match — and it over-reads.

A word match cannot tell whether the word was about the place. A passage that merely MENTIONS a place while describing something else answers several slots at once, from words that were never about the place: in specimen 1 (Argonath) one sentence answers MATERIAL, CONDITION, FIGURES — on the words stone, unbreakable, men; in specimen 2 (Underharrow) one sentence answers FORM, SCALE, SETTING, FIGURES — on the words doors, great, road, men; in specimen 3 (Gates of Sirion) one sentence answers FORM, SCALE, SETTING — on the words gates, great, hills.

Measured, not impressionistic: 9 of the 141 built places have three or more slots answered by a SINGLE passage — one sentence doing several slots’ work, which is what over-reading looks like from outside.

Nothing on this page is published and nothing is a prompt yet. This is what a purely mechanical corpus-first prompt produces, shown so you can rule on whether the form needs a hand between the corpus and the prompt — reading each passage and saying what it is actually about — or whether the passages printed beside every detail are enough. That is the question C253 exists to answer, and it is the one thing this page cannot decide for you.
