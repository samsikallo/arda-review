A lost folio

The leaf you asked for is not in this book. What follows is what the archive knows about its absence, which is more than an apology.

A codex loses leaves. A folio comes away at the fold, a gathering is bound out of order, and a reader who has the reference in front of them turns to a place where the text is not.a That is this page: not a failure of the archive so much as a lacuna in it, and the archive prints its lacunae rather than hiding them.

Why a leaf goes missing here

Three reasons, and only the third is a fault. A route may have been retired — superseded by a hall that does its work better, in which case its own leaf is cut out and its successor named on the facing page, where this archive keeps 6 of them. A route may never have existed, and a bookmark or a typed address invented it. Or the binding is genuinely wrong, and a page that should be here is not.

The first is the commonest and the least alarming: a retirement here is a transfer of work, not a deletion of it. The second the book cannot tell from the third, and does not pretend to — the manifest carries no alias table, so an address names a leaf exactly or names nothing — a near miss is reported as a miss and never resolved to a neighbour.b

The archive's own rule — a silence is a MEASUREMENT, never a fall-through. This leaf cannot tell you the archive holds nothing on your subject, only that this address holds nothing.c
What the binding refuses to do

Two refusals are written into the machinery that places every leaf, quoted here in its own words. It will not inventing a volume for an unplaced route — “the tool exits non-zero instead, so 'every route is placed' is a checkable fact and not a sentence somebody wrote.” It will not prev/next across the entity records — “alphabetical adjacency is not a reading sequence; an arrow promising one would lie.”

The book carries 649 leaves in 7 volumes, and the search on the front board reaches every one of them. [C]

Where to turn instead is a question the book can answer exactly, because the order of its volumes is not a menu somebody typed but the binding itself.e The seven fore-edge tabs above lead to the seven contents leaves, each listing what its volume holds.

The seven volumes
  • Atlas and RealmsI
  • Annals, Wars and ReckoningsII
  • Peoples and HousesIII
  • Living World, Relics and DevicesIV
  • Tongues, Scripts, Names and VerseV
  • Lore and First PrinciplesVI
  • Index, Corpus and Reader's PathsVII

Each volume is one question, and the question is the volume's own line in the manifest rather than a description of it: Volume I asks where things are; II, when things happened; III, who they were; IV, what was made and what grows; V, how they spoke and wrote; VI, what holds the world up; VII, the apparatus of the archive. A reader who has lost a reference and does not know its volume should begin at the last of those.

Nor do they carry equal weight: Volume III holds 436 of the book's leaves and Volume II holds 4. Across all seven stand 431 people, 91 places, 60 realms, and 43 further leaves about the archive itself rather than about Arda — its contents, its index, its corpus and its workbenches.
